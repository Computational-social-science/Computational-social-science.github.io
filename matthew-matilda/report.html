Can the name histories of Merton and Zuckerman identify a Matthew or a Matilda effect?

Replication report · 20 November 2026. The V2.6 working study asked whether scientists who were already prominent tend to receive credit that belongs to someone else. A narrower question can be answered with its paired experiment. Merton's name was more visible in English books on the two comparable short-name specifications around 1968. The size and even the direction of change in that disparity depend on the measure. To find out whether credit moved between people requires an observation about who did something and a second observation about whom a later source credited for that same act.

What was actually drawn

The paired workbook contains three observed–fitted pairs, each with 320 rows. Both sheets have exactly the same observed Merton column. Their fitted Merton columns differ in all 320 rows (maximum absolute difference 0.0803 in workbook units). The original code plotted the supplied fits as lines and shaded observed values down to −0.01. That lower boundary is an arbitrary graphical baseline. Reading the filled region as an uncertainty interval would give it a statistical meaning absent from the code.

The year labels can be recovered independently. All 320 Merton values match the dated target in the author's public workbook after conversion to single precision. Its first year is 1700. Zero-based index 268 therefore denotes 1968. A separate reconstruction using English 2019, a centred seven-year window and series-specific min–max scaling agrees within 0.000001 at 319 of 320 rows for each initialed name. The discrepancies are in 2019 (0.0282 for Merton and 0.00253 for Zuckerman). These are material when claiming exact replication. Repeating the measurement in current English

We fetched Google's current English corpus with selector en. The Viewer release notes identify the July 2024 dataset. We kept annual, unsmoothed, exact-case values for 1500–2022 and retained the source responses and hashes. Six requested name strings yield 3,138 query-years. Of these, 480 are positive, 2,135 are returned zeros and 523 are missing because Google returned no series for the Academy's recorded full name “Harriet Anne Zuckerman”. Google returned “Harriet A. Zuckerman” and “Harriet Zuckerman”, both used by Columbia. The corpus does not identify individual books or provide the raw annual counts behind these particular responses. For string q in year t, the frequency F(q,t) equals the count of q divided by the annual count of all n-grams of its order. For a window W of paired returned years, let M and Z be the arithmetic means of F(q,t) for the two selected strings. We report M − Z (multiplied by one billion when labelled per billion) and M/Z. Each year has equal weight. A zero denominator or no paired years leaves the ratio undefined. The Viewer does not expose its token order for the initialed labels, so those series are described by their exact strings. Forms are kept separate to avoid double-counting or silently switching the population of strings. References to the economist Robert C. Merton or to unrelated people are a potential source of ambiguity for short forms.

Both strings gained visibility. The difference widened as Merton's absolute gain was larger, while the ratio narrowed as Zuckerman's proportional gain was larger. Treating either pattern as a verdict on which scientist gained credit would make that verdict depend on the scale chosen. Around 1968, the five-year initialed ratio also falls (425.1 to 200.8). Around the 1964 printed naming report, the five-year initialed ratio is undefined before the event. The ten-year results are available alongside the five-year results. These overlapping, descriptive windows omit the focal year. Book publication lags, prior career histories, language coverage, and the absence of a parallel comparison group prevent an intervention estimate.

How do we validate a measure of scientific fame?

The starting point is a person known to a particular audience in a particular period. The book corpus supplies a written trace of that visibility. In the original English release, the 201 annual word denominators for 1800–2000 agree with Michel et al.’s supplementary workbook. In the current English Viewer the available outputs are normalized query fractions. A word-denominated, entity-resolved rate needs a corpus with auditable text. Measurement protocol and validation status

The three Zuckerman forms belong to the same person. Google’s requested three-name sum reproduced the two-word spelling alone in every one of 86 years (1937–2022), under both smoothing settings. At smoothing zero, adding the two individually returned series instead differs from the displayed sum in 59 years. The expanded name is missing when queried alone. Life-stage comparison changes the question. At annual age indices 26–30, the five-year mean displayed fraction for “Robert K. Merton” was 1.062 × 10⁻⁸ (1936–1940). For “Harriet Zuckerman” it was 7.498 × 10⁻¹⁰ (1963–1967). These exact strings have different structures and the calendar periods differ by 27 years. At the date of the 19 May 1964 report the two were 53 and 26. Both age and calendar time are available in the explorer.

A demonstration used complete selected pages in six independent works. In the 1968 paper, pp. 56 and 58 supply 2,147 extracted word tokens, one registered mention of Merton and three of Zuckerman. Each has one represented work out of one, although their mention totals differ threefold. In another year, Scholes’s lecture provides two qualified mentions of the economist on the selected pages and removing that work leaves the year without observations. The preselected material tests counting, ambiguity and sensitivity. It cannot tell us how many books in the publishing population mentioned either scientist. Page-panel measurements (CSV)

We next drew 200 catalog works independently of target names and availability, 20 per year in 1959–1963 and 1969–1973. The exact-edition archive check returned 5 OCR candidates, 55 restricted items, 132 without an exact locator and 8 with mismatched or unconfirmed editions. The five scans yielded zero candidate hits across 415,174 OCR words. Ten randomly selected images yielded zero independently identified target mentions. Precision and recall are undefined here. Removing a multiyear compilation reduced the provisional count to 274,520 words in four scans. Treating the other works as zero would erase the main finding about access. Which hypothesis can the case test?

Prior prominence can influence recognition; recognition can in turn increase later name frequency. Role, seniority, research topic and access to the original source can influence both visibility and attribution. A gender-linked discount and prestige-based reinforcement can coexist. One pair ties gender, seniority, individual identity and several contribution roles together. Observing more years does not break that dependence. Conversely, finding a small frequency gap would not vindicate an incorrect assignment in an actual source. Rossiter's formulation motivates testing under-recognition in acts of attribution. Merton (1968) · Rossiter (1993)

A role-sensitive test starts with the 1968 paper's acknowledgment of Zuckerman's interviews, the contemporaneous 1964 naming report, and the parties' later accounts of formulation and authorship. For each later passage, record whether its subject gives an opportunity to report the chosen role (including accounts with an unnamed actor). For eligible readable passages distinguish accurate attribution, omission, explicit assignment to someone else, and ambiguity. Attribute outcomes using the words in the passage and information available at the time. Estimate a transfer proportion as explicit transfers divided by eligible assessed passages with a prespecified treatment of ambiguity. Disclose that count, ambiguity, and unread material. Report role-specific omission separately. Link editions and shared antecedents before pooling observations. A population estimate would also need a sampling design and a missingness analysis.

In the frozen OpenAlex frame of 69 citing works (1968–1979), the first six chronological entries have no verified passage. In a separate six-work access comparison, four have verified passages. Mittermeir and Knorr (1979, p. 235) explicitly attribute the name to Merton. Knorr et al. (1979, p. 56) attribute the proposal of the theoretical account. Wilson and Yankauer use the mechanism. Only the first passage gives a naming opportunity (one accurate assignment). None gives an opportunity to assess who collected the 1968 paper's interview evidence, leaving that proportion undefined. Shared authorship links the two chapters; Yankauer cites Wilson. Treating all four as four independent endorsements would overstate the evidence.
